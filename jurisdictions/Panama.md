**Bitcoin Regulation, Self-Custody & Sui Generis Treatment**

## Executive Summary

Panama currently operates without a dedicated virtual-asset or VASP licensing statute. Crypto-asset activity is legal. The primary binding obligations for service providers remain anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing rules under Law 23 of 2015, supervised by the Unidad de Análisis Financiero (UAF). There is no crypto-specific licence to obtain and no specialised crypto regulator issuing authorisations.

Multiple legislative proposals have been introduced (notably Bills 247 and 326 of 2025, and Anteproyecto / Proyecto de Ley linked to N° 314 / 487 of early 2026). None has been enacted as of mid-August 2026. Earlier efforts (including the 2022 bill) failed after partial executive objection and constitutional challenge.

Recent public positioning — including statements highlighted by JAN3 regarding municipal Bitcoin tax payments in Panama City, proposals concerning Canal-related payments, legislative work by deputies, and commentary from the Sovereign Wealth Fund’s Chief Investment Officer — frames Panama as an emerging candidate for Bitcoin-linked economic activity centred on its trade and logistics corridor. These developments remain policy and political signals rather than enacted law.

Self-custody is not restricted. The absence of a licensing regime means pure holding and peer-to-peer activity sit outside any specialised authorisation requirement.

## 1. Historical Thread

- **2021–2022**: Bill 697 attempted to create a crypto framework. Approved by the National Assembly; partially objected to by the executive; later rendered inapplicable by the Supreme Court on constitutional grounds.
- **2025**: New legislative efforts (Bill 247 and Bill 326) introduced, focusing on digital-asset recognition, VASP registration, FATF alignment, and a proposed National Council of Digital Assets.
- **January 2026**: Anteproyecto de Ley N° 314 (later associated with Proyecto de Ley N° 487), a broader fintech framework that would define VASPs/CASPs, introduce licensing, capital and governance standards, and place supervision with financial regulators and the UAF. Still pending first debate / enactment as of mid-2026.
- **May 2026**: Law No. 526 introduces economic-substance rules for certain multinational entities earning foreign-source passive income (relevant to structuring but not crypto-specific).
- **Ongoing**: Superintendencia del Mercado de Valores (SMV) has stated that cryptocurrencies are not automatically securities under existing securities law; substance-over-form analysis applies to specific products.

## 2. Current Regulatory Overview

### Licensing and Status of Crypto Activity
- No dedicated VASP or CASP licence exists.
- Crypto businesses typically operate through ordinary Panamanian companies (Sociedad Anónima) with an Aviso de Operación and, where required, UAF registration as obligated subjects under AML/CFT rules.
- Claims of a “Panama crypto licence” do not correspond to any formal statutory regime currently in force.

### AML/CFT
Law 23 of 2015 remains the principal binding framework. Service providers that fall within the definition of obligated subjects must implement AML programmes, appoint compliance officers, and meet UAF requirements.

### Securities Treatment
The SMV has indicated that cryptocurrencies are not treated as securities or financial instruments by default. Specific tokenised products or platforms may still be analysed under securities rules if they exhibit securities-like characteristics.

### Taxation
No comprehensive crypto-specific tax statute is in force. Ordinary income-tax and capital-gains principles apply according to the nature of the activity and the taxpayer’s status. Draft bills have contemplated special tax treatment, but these remain non-binding.

### Self-Custody
Self-custody of Bitcoin is not prohibited. In the absence of a licensing regime, pure self-custodial holding and peer-to-peer transfers do not trigger specialised authorisation requirements.

### Municipal and Canal-Related Signals
Public commentary (including references to Panama City municipal tax payments in Bitcoin and proposals concerning Canal transit fees) indicates political interest in practical Bitcoin use-cases tied to Panama’s logistics and trade infrastructure. These remain policy initiatives rather than nationwide legal tender or mandatory acceptance rules.

## 4. Philosophical & Legal Synthesis

Panama currently occupies a “legal but lightly regulated” position. This creates both opportunity and uncertainty. The absence of a dedicated licensing statute means self-custody and non-intermediated activity face fewer formal barriers than in jurisdictions with comprehensive VASP regimes. At the same time, the repeated introduction of draft frameworks (and the failure of earlier attempts) signals that the status quo is not expected to remain static, particularly in light of upcoming FATF evaluation pressures.

From the framework’s perspective the critical points are:

- Bitcoin remains capable of being held and transferred outside licensed intermediaries.
- Any future VASP regime should be assessed for its treatment of non-custodial software, pure self-custody, and the distinction between intermediation and personal use.
- Panama’s strategic interest in linking digital assets to its trade corridor and logistics infrastructure is a political and economic signal worth monitoring, but it does not yet alter the legal baseline for self-custody or classification.

## 5. Adoption Implications

- Self-custody is currently unconstrained by specialised crypto licensing.
- Service providers must still navigate AML/CFT obligations under Law 23/2015.
- Future legislation (particularly any enacted version of the 2025–2026 fintech/VASP drafts) could introduce formal licensing, capital, and governance requirements.
- Political signals around municipal payments and Canal-related use-cases suggest openness to practical Bitcoin integration, but these have not yet crystallised into binding national rules.

**Truth & Clarity Summary**  
Panama has no dedicated crypto licensing regime in force as of August 2026. Activity is legal; the main obligations are AML/CFT under Law 23 of 2015. Multiple draft frameworks remain pending. Self-custody is not restricted. Political and institutional signals (municipal payments, legislative proposals, Sovereign Wealth Fund commentary) position Panama as an emerging candidate for Bitcoin-linked economic activity, particularly around its trade corridor, but these signals have not yet become binding nationwide law.
