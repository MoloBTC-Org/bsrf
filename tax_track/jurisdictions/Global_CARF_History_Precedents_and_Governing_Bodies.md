## 1. Purpose of this document

CARF (Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework) is the latest major extension of a multi-decade project to create standardised, automatic exchange of taxpayer financial information between jurisdictions. It applies the same institutional architecture previously used against traditional offshore banking and investment structures to Reporting Crypto-Asset Service Providers (RCASPs / CASPs).

This document maps:

- The historical sequence of frameworks that preceded CARF.
- The principal intergovernmental bodies that designed and enforced them.
- The consistent pattern of moving from “information on request” to automatic, multilateral exchange, then expanding the class of intermediaries required to report.

---

## 2. Pre-CARF historical sequence (traditional finance)

**1970 – United States Bank Secrecy Act**  
Early domestic reporting regime requiring financial institutions to report large cash transactions and certain foreign accounts (FBAR later developed from this foundation). Marked the beginning of systematic domestic and cross-border financial reporting pressure.

**1996–1998 – OECD Harmful Tax Competition work**  
G7 (Lyon 1996) instructed the OECD to examine “harmful tax competition”. The 1998 OECD report *Harmful Tax Competition: An Emerging Global Issue* identified tax havens and preferential regimes characterised by low/no tax, lack of effective exchange of information, and lack of transparency. This report is the foundational political and analytical document for the subsequent transparency agenda.

**2000 – Original Global Forum**  
Established under OECD auspices as a body bringing together OECD members and certain non-members (including many traditional offshore financial centres) to promote transparency and exchange of information on request.

**2009 – Restructuring of the Global Forum**  
In the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis and high-profile tax scandals, the G20 declared that “the era of banking secrecy is over”. The Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes was fundamentally restructured, opened to all jurisdictions on an equal footing, and given a peer-review mandate. Membership expanded rapidly. This is the institutional vehicle that later oversaw both CRS and CARF implementation monitoring.

**2010 – United States FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act)**  
Unilateral US legislation requiring foreign financial institutions to report accounts held by US persons or face withholding tax. Implemented primarily through intergovernmental agreements (IGAs). FATCA demonstrated both the technical model (due diligence + automatic reporting) and the geopolitical leverage available to a major financial centre. It is widely acknowledged as the direct template for the later multilateral CRS.

**2013–2014 – OECD Common Reporting Standard (CRS)**  
G20 formally requested the OECD to develop a global automatic exchange standard. The CRS was approved in 2014. It requires financial institutions in participating jurisdictions to identify accounts held by non-residents and report specified information annually to their local tax authority, which then automatically exchanges it with the residence jurisdiction of the account holder. First exchanges began in 2017 for early adopters. Over 100 jurisdictions eventually committed.

**Parallel EU track**  
The EU developed its own series of Directives on Administrative Cooperation (DAC). DAC2 implemented CRS within the EU; later DAC iterations expanded reporting obligations. DAC8 (2023) is the EU vehicle for implementing CARF rules from 2026.

## 3. Emergence of CARF specifically

**2021**  
G20 Finance Ministers (April 2021) acknowledged progress on tax transparency standards and explicitly supported OECD work exploring automatic exchange of information on crypto-assets.

**2022**  
OECD, working with G20 countries, completed the core rules of the Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework. Public consultation ran March–April 2022. The framework deliberately mirrors the architecture of the CRS but adapts it to crypto-asset intermediaries and transaction types (exchanges, transfers, certain payments, etc.).

**October 2022**  
OECD published the final CARF rules together with amendments to the CRS.

**June 2023**  
OECD Council adopted the revised Recommendation on the International Standards for Automatic Exchange of Information in Tax Matters, incorporating both the amended CRS and CARF. G20 Leaders subsequently called for swift and widespread implementation and asked the Global Forum to ensure coordinated timelines.

**2023–2026**  
Global Forum established a dedicated CARF Group. Jurisdictions began domestic legislative processes and signed the Multilateral Competent Authority Agreement on Automatic Exchange of Information pursuant to the CARF (CARF MCAA). First data collection in many jurisdictions (including South Africa and EU members via DAC8) commenced in 2026, with first international exchanges targeted for 2027–2029 depending on the jurisdiction.

**Political champions**

- Primary institutional designer and technical author: OECD Committee on Fiscal Affairs and its Working Party No. 10, operating under G20 political mandate.
- Political impetus: successive G20 Finance Ministers and Leaders’ communiqués from 2021 onwards.
- Implementation monitoring and peer pressure: Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes.
- EU parallel track: European Commission (DAC8).
- United States: committed to later exchanges (2029) while already operating its own domestic crypto reporting rules.

## 4. Governing bodies and the institutional pattern (five decades)

| Period | Primary body / instrument | Core function |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------|
| 1970s–1990s | US Treasury / IRS; early OECD work | Domestic reporting + bilateral information exchange |
| 1996–2000 | OECD (Harmful Tax Competition project); G7 | Definition of “harmful” regimes and tax havens; pressure for transparency |
| 2000–2009 | Original Global Forum (OECD-hosted) | Soft coordination of information exchange on request |
| 2009–present | Restructured Global Forum (G20-mandated, OECD-supported) | Peer review of Exchange of Information on Request (EOIR) and later Automatic Exchange of Information (AEOI) |
| 2010 | US Congress / Treasury (FATCA) | Unilateral automatic reporting model with extraterritorial reach |
| 2013–2014 | OECD + G20 (CRS) | Multilateralisation of the FATCA-style automatic exchange model |
| 2021–2023 | OECD + G20 (CARF) | Extension of the same automatic-exchange architecture to crypto-asset service providers |
| Parallel | FATF (Financial Action Task Force) | AML/CFT standards that reinforce transparency pressure (beneficial ownership, travel rule, etc.) |
| Parallel | European Union (DAC series) | Regional binding implementation of OECD standards |

The consistent institutional pattern is:

1. Political mandate from G7/G20.  
2. Technical design by the OECD Committee on Fiscal Affairs.  
3. Monitoring, peer review and commitment processes run by the Global Forum.  
4. Domestic transposition by individual jurisdictions (sometimes accelerated by EU directives).  
5. Progressive expansion of the class of reporting entities and of the data elements exchanged.

## 5. Condensed timeline

- 1970 – US Bank Secrecy Act  
- 1998 – OECD Harmful Tax Competition report  
- 2000 – Global Forum established  
- 2009 – Global Forum restructured under G20 pressure; “era of banking secrecy is over”  
- 2010 – US FATCA enacted  
- 2014 – OECD CRS finalised and endorsed by G20  
- 2017 – First CRS automatic exchanges  
- 2021 – G20 calls for work on crypto-asset transparency  
- 2022 – OECD publishes CARF rules after public consultation  
- 2023 – OECD adopts CARF + amended CRS as International Standards; G20 calls for swift implementation  
- 2026 – Data collection begins in first-wave jurisdictions (including South Africa and EU)  
- 2027–2029 – Staggered first international exchanges under CARF

## 6. Relevance to sovereignty analysis (observational)

CARF continues the established trajectory of requiring regulated intermediaries (now crypto-asset service providers rather than traditional banks or custodians) to collect and report specified information on non-resident users for automatic exchange with tax authorities. 

Pure self-custody activity that never interacts with a Reporting Crypto-Asset Service Provider falls outside the direct reporting obligations of the framework itself, in the same way that holding physical cash or bearer instruments historically fell outside the core CRS/FATCA reporting net. 

The practical effect depends on the extent to which users interact with reporting intermediaries and on domestic rules layered on top of the international standard.

### 6.1 Contemporary instruments that continue the same institutional pattern

In addition to CARF itself, the following instruments form the current global tax-information and transparency portfolio against which self-custody boundaries must be assessed:

- EU Directive on Administrative Cooperation (DAC8)
- United States Internal Revenue Code broker-reporting rules (including Form 1099-DA)
- Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes peer-review and commitment processes
- Bilateral Double Tax Agreements (exchange-of-information articles)
- FATF Recommendations on beneficial ownership and the Travel Rule (as reinforcing pressure)

These instruments remain intermediary-centric. Pure self-custody activity that never touches a Reporting Crypto-Asset Service Provider or equivalent regulated intermediary continues to fall outside their direct reporting obligations.
